# Antecedent conditions: soil moisture, baseflow, and wetness proxies

*Corpus entry, 2026-08-24. Part of the Cascadia Papsukkal flood-science corpus.*

Domain lead scope: the quantified effect of antecedent catchment state on flood magnitude; which
antecedent proxies carry skill and how they rank against each other; whether a day-of-year
streamflow percentile is a defensible substitute for soil moisture; seasonal wet-up in maritime
climates; and how antecedent state is represented in NWRFC/HEFS/ESP and the National Water Model.
Every claim is labelled FACT (a source was fetched and read, or a dataset was pulled and computed
here), INFERENCE, ASSUMPTION or OPEN QUESTION.

---

## 1. Headline

**Antecedent wetness is a large multiplier on flood response almost everywhere on the U.S. West
Coast — and western Washington is one of the places where it is *least* informative, because the
basins are already at the top of their storage range for most of the flood season.** The
peer-reviewed West Coast synthesis (Webb et al. 2025) explicitly classifies Washington and the
interior ranges as the low-sensitivity group; two independent lines of primary evidence computed
for this entry agree: (a) at Cayuse Pass SNOTEL, 96.1 % of all November–February days over 15
years sit within 2 percentage points of the station's record-maximum 20-inch soil moisture, with a
November–February interquartile range of **0.50 percentage points**; (b) across the six gauges
Cascadia Papsukkal actually reads, the day-of-year streamflow percentile has a Spearman
correlation of only 0.46–0.71 with the following week's maximum flow, and raises the probability
of a top-1 % Nov–Feb flow from a 1.0 % base rate to only **2.1–5.1 %**.

The operational consequence is sharp and specific. Replaying Event Zero with a climatology that
excludes 2025, `method:susceptibility-index@0.1.0` would have read **LOW in four of six basins on
3–4 December 2025 and MODERATE in all six on 5 December** — the day NWS Seattle issued its first
Flood Watch, 6.5 days before the record Mount Vernon crest. It first read VERY HIGH on 8–10
December, when the rivers were already in flood. Across the full record, **45–76 % of all VERY
HIGH readings occur on days when flow is already more than 25 % above its value three days
earlier.** The surface as built is predominantly a *concurrent high-flow detector*, not an
antecedent-state estimator. It should be kept — it is honest about being an index and it is the
only wetness signal the platform can compute today — but it must be relabelled, given a
rate-of-change term, and joined by a real storage proxy, which is available now and unbuilt: the
NWM v3.0 retrospective carries 44 years of 1-km volumetric soil moisture on public S3.

## 2. Mechanisms — the physics, stated properly

### 2.1 The state variable is a storage *deficit*, and the response to it is a threshold

Runoff in humid forested catchments is generated by saturation excess on expanding variable source
areas and by lateral subsurface stormflow, not by infiltration excess (FACT — established in the
repo's own prior pass, `flood-genesis-mechanisms-2026-08-24.md` §1.2, on H.J. Andrews evidence).
The controlling quantity is therefore how much storage remains before the subsurface flow network
*connects*.

Tromp-van Meerveld & McDonnell (2006) measured that threshold directly: across 147 storms on the
Panola hillslope, subsurface stormflow was negligible below a **55 mm** precipitation threshold and
**more than 75× larger** once connectivity was achieved (FACT, via the prior pass). McDonnell et
al. (2021) generalised this as *fill-and-spill*. Critically, the 55 mm is not a property of the
storm: it is the storage deficit of the bedrock microtopography, which antecedent conditions set.
The same storm is sub-threshold on a drained hillslope and super-threshold on a primed one.

The consequence for representation is structural, not cosmetic: **the honest form of an antecedent
index is a distance to a threshold, not a linear rank.** A percentile band implies a smooth,
monotone dose-response that the physics does not have.

### 2.2 Why streamflow is a legitimate — and imperfect — window onto storage

Kirchner (2009, *WRR*, "Catchments as simple dynamical systems") gives the theoretical licence for
using discharge as a storage proxy. For the class of catchments in which discharge is determined
by the volume of water in storage, the catchment is a first-order nonlinear dynamical system:

```
dS/dt = P − E − Q          (mass balance)
Q     = f(S)               (single-valued storage–discharge relation)
g(Q)  ≡ dQ/dS = f'(f⁻¹(Q)) (the "sensitivity function" — how sharply Q responds to a change in S)
```

Because `f` is monotone it is invertible, so `dQ/dS` can be written as a function of `Q` alone and
inferred from streamflow recessions with no other data. Kirchner shows this yields quantitative
estimates of **catchment dynamic storage, recession time scales, and sensitivity to antecedent
moisture** (FACT — abstract fetched via OpenAlex; the Wiley full text returned HTTP 403 and was not
independently read).

Two consequences follow directly, and they are the intellectual core of this entry:

1. **A percentile of Q is a monotone transform of a percentile of S.** If `Q = f(S)` with `f`
   monotone, then rank(Q) = rank(S). A day-of-year *flow* percentile is therefore, under
   Kirchner's assumption, a day-of-year *storage* percentile. This is a real theoretical
   justification for the platform's method (INFERENCE from Kirchner 2009).
2. **The assumption that `f` is single-valued is the weak link.** The empirical literature is
   consistent that storage–discharge relations are **hysteretic**: discharge depends not only on
   current storage but on the history of wetting and drying, with storage rising on one trajectory
   during wetting and falling on another during recession (FACT — Hydrological Processes,
   "Hysteresis and scale in catchment storage, flow, and transport", abstract read via search
   result; the full text was not independently fetched). Documented causes include antecedent
   storage-deficit profiles, preferential flow paths, changing contributing areas between wetting
   and drying, changing flow-path connectivity, and fill-and-spill itself.

   INFERENCE, and it is the platform's single largest methodological exposure: **on the rising limb
   the flow percentile systematically understates storage, and on the recession limb it
   systematically overstates it.** A basin can be near its connectivity threshold while its river is
   still low, which is precisely the state in which a susceptibility surface would earn its keep.

### 2.3 The recursive proxies: API, SPI/SSPI, and the soil-moisture-accounting bucket

**Antecedent Precipitation Index** (Kohler & Linsley 1951), the classical recency-weighted store:

```
API_j = k · API_{j−1} + P_j          0 < k < 1, k calibrated
```

`k` is the daily depletion factor; `k = 0.98` implies an e-folding memory of ≈ 50 days, `k = 0.90`
≈ 9.5 days (INFERENCE, arithmetic: −1/ln k).

**Standardized Precipitation Index** at an accumulation window *n*: fit a Gamma distribution to the
historical record of rolling *n*-day precipitation totals, then map the current total to a standard
normal deviate. Webb et al. (2026) use a *seasonal* variant, **SSPI**: the Gamma reference is fit
only to rolling 90-day totals **ending during the cool season**, preserving seasonal context while
remaining comparable between years and regions (FACT — Webb et al. 2026, methods, fetched via PMC).

**Soil Moisture Accounting (SMA) bucket**: a production store of capacity `A` filled by daily
precipitation and drained by evaporation — in Tramblay et al. (2012) the GR4J production reservoir
without percolation. This is the family that NWS SAC-SMA belongs to.

### 2.4 Why maritime basins pin at the top of their range in winter

The seasonal water balance explains the null result that dominates §5. In western Washington,
November–February precipitation vastly exceeds potential evapotranspiration, so the soil column is
driven to and held at field capacity: `dS/dt = P − E − Q` with `P ≫ E` leaves `S` at the value
where `f(S)` can pass essentially all input. Storage variance collapses; only the *deficit
accumulated over summer* and the *speed of autumn refill* carry information.

That refill has a measured timescale for this exact landscape: in a 60-ha forested watershed in the
western Cascades of Oregon, the soil moisture deficit accumulated by transpiration took **an
average of 48 days to refill after the onset of winter rains**, and soil moisture explained **more
than 80 % of the variation in streamflow at every temporal scale investigated**, with the strongest
soil-moisture–streamflow coupling in the wet season (FACT — Moore, Jones & Bond 2011, *Hydrological
Processes* 25(24):3701–3710, abstract and results fetched from USFS Treesearch).

INFERENCE: 48 days from the onset of sustained autumn rain puts a typical western-Cascade basin at
its winter storage plateau by mid-to-late November. The window in which antecedent moisture is
genuinely discriminating in this region is therefore roughly **mid-September to mid-November, plus
the aftermath of an anomalously dry autumn, plus March–May** — not the November–January flood peak.

### 2.5 The extremeness threshold: antecedent state matters *less* as events get larger

This is the qualification most often lost when the "2–4.5×" number is quoted.

- Wasko et al. (2020, *WRR*, 221 Australian catchments): "the link between flood, soil moisture, and
  rainfall timing is confounded by event severity: for less extreme events flood timing is more
  likely to correspond to soil moisture timing, whereas **rainfall timing becomes increasingly
  important as flood severity increases**" (FACT — abstract fetched via OpenAlex).
- Brunner et al. (2021, *Communications Earth & Environment*, 78 Bavarian catchments): a **return
  interval threshold** exists in the precipitation–flood relation; above it, increases in extreme
  precipitation clearly yield increased flood magnitudes, while **below it flood magnitude is
  modulated by land surface processes** (FACT — abstract fetched via OpenAlex).
- Kundzewicz et al. (2013) and Sharma et al. (2018) frame the same tension at continental scale:
  rising extreme precipitation has not produced correspondingly rising flood peaks, and antecedent
  soil moisture is the leading explanation (FACT for the framing; the Sharma paper was not
  independently fetched).

INFERENCE for Cascadia Papsukkal: the platform's job is the upper tail. That is exactly the regime
in which antecedent state has the least marginal information — and it is compounded by the
maritime pinning of §2.4. Two independent effects push the same way.

### 2.6 How antecedent state is represented in the systems that issue the official forecast

**NWRFC (the authority for every Cascadia Papsukkal basin).** Forecasts are produced in the
Community Hydrologic Prediction System (CHPS). Each basin is divided into elevation zones; each
zone runs mean areal forcings → a rain/snow operation → **SNOW-17** → **SAC-SMA**, with runoff
converted to flow and routed by unit hydrograph and, where applicable, a reservoir model
(SSARRESV). The model state vector is explicitly **snow water equivalent, snow cover, soil moisture,
and river/reservoir levels**. The forecaster interactively adjusts the model so the simulation
matches observations before the forecast is run forward. Ensemble Streamflow Prediction takes those
*current* model states and drives them with an ensemble of historical weather years (1948–2015),
producing 67 traces (FACT — NWRFC ESP webinar deck, `nwrfc.noaa.gov/presentations/html/wy2016_ws/ESP_Description_Webinar_20160518.pdf`,
fetched and read 2026-08-24).

Two things follow. First, **the antecedent state that matters operationally in western Washington is
a SAC-SMA state vector inside CHPS**, not a satellite or in-situ soil moisture measurement. Second,
that state is not published as a public gridded product; the platform cannot read it. What it can
read — the NWRFC/NWPS deterministic forecast — already *contains* the antecedent state, adjusted by
a human. This is a strong argument for the repo's existing ordering of authority
(`HYDROLOGY.md` §5): the official forecast is the best available antecedent-state-conditioned
product in the region.

**National Water Model.** WRF-Hydro/Noah-MP land surface, with analysis-and-assimilation cycles
that **nudge to more than 6,000 USGS streamflow observations**; other configurations run open-loop.
Soil moisture states are *not* assimilated operationally — streamflow nudging is the only
operational DA (FACT for streamflow nudging and the count; INFERENCE, from the absence of any
operational soil-moisture DA in the retrieved material, for the negative claim. Not independently
verified against an NWM configuration document).

INFERENCE: because NWM soil moisture is open-loop with respect to soil observations, an NWM
soil-saturation percentile is a *model* statement about the water balance driven by AORC/analysis
precipitation. That is exactly what the platform's DATA_DOCTRINE calls MODELED, and it is
defensible so long as it is badged as such and compared against its own retrospective climatology
rather than against an absolute saturation value.
